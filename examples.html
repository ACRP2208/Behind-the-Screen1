Examples

Well-known cases that were already verified by journalists, scientists or authorities.

FALSE

"COVID-19 vaccines contain microchips"

This rumor, which blamed Bill Gates, spread on social media during the pandemic. Vaccines do not contain microchips or tracking devices, and manufacturers and health agencies publish their ingredients.

How to spot it: no scientific source supports it, and it plays on fear.

TRUE

"Current climate change is mainly caused by human activity"

This is the conclusion of the scientific consensus. Organizations such as the IPCC and NASA state that burning fossil fuels is the main cause of recent warming.

How to check it: many independent studies and scientific bodies support it.

MISLEADING

"The 1938 War of the Worlds radio broadcast caused mass panic"

Orson Welles’ adaptation did air and some listeners were alarmed, but historians say the mass panic was exaggerated by newspapers of the time, which saw radio as a competitor.

How to spot it: a real event told with more drama than it had.

FALSE

"Drinking bleach or disinfectant cures COVID-19"

This is false and dangerous. Health authorities warned that swallowing these products causes serious poisoning and does not protect against the virus.

How to spot it: miracle cures with no clinical studies are a red flag.

TRUE

"Humans reached the Moon in 1969"

Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. Moon rocks have been studied by scientists in several countries, and laser reflectors left there are still used to measure the distance to the Moon.

How to check it: there is physical evidence that independent teams can verify.

MISLEADING

"Albert Einstein failed math at school"

Einstein did very well in math from a young age. The story likely grew from an entrance exam he did not pass at 16 for a Swiss university, but he failed the non-science sections and scored very high in math and physics.

How to spot it: a surprising claim about a famous person with no primary source.

FALSE

"Pope Francis endorsed Donald Trump in 2016"

A website imitating a news outlet published this story during the U.S. election and it spread widely on Facebook. The Vatican made no such endorsement.

How to spot it: the site was unknown and no serious outlet reported it.

TRUE

"Smoking causes lung cancer"

Decades of research, including major reports such as the 1964 U.S. Surgeon General’s report, established the link, and health agencies around the world confirm it.

How to check it: the evidence comes from many independent studies over many years.

MISLEADING

"Napoleon was extremely short"

He was about 1.68 m (around 5 ft 6 in), average or slightly above for a Frenchman of his time. The myth comes partly from confusion between French and English units and from British cartoons that mocked him.

How to spot it: a measurement that changes depending on the unit used.

FALSE

"5G towers spread COVID-19"

Health agencies, including the WHO, say viruses cannot travel on radio waves or mobile networks, and COVID-19 spread in countries with no 5G. The rumor led to attacks on phone masts in several countries.

How to spot it: it links two unrelated things that happened at the same time.

TRUE

"Smallpox has been eradicated"

The World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated in 1980 after a global vaccination campaign. It was the first human disease ever eradicated.

How to check it: confirmed by the WHO and health authorities worldwide.

MISLEADING

A real photo of a past disaster shared as if it happened today

The photo is real, but it comes from an older event. This is one of the most common tricks: a real image with a new, false caption. Searching for the image’s origin usually reveals the earlier date.

How to spot it: use reverse image search (see TinEye and Google Lens in Where to verify) and check the date.

FALSE

"We only use 10% of our brain"

Brain imaging shows that nearly all regions of the brain are active over a day, and damage to almost any area has effects. The idea is a popular myth with no scientific support.

How to spot it: a catchy number with no source is a warning sign.

TRUE

"The Titanic sank in 1912 after hitting an iceberg"

The ship struck an iceberg on the night of 14 April 1912 in the North Atlantic and sank early the next day. Its wreck was found in 1985 and has been explored many times.

How to check it: official inquiries, survivor accounts and the wreck itself agree.

MISLEADING

"Lemmings jump off cliffs in mass suicide"

Lemmings do migrate in large numbers and some drown crossing water, but they do not kill themselves on purpose. The famous scene in a 1958 nature documentary was staged by the filmmakers, according to later investigations.

How to spot it: even documentaries and well-known brands can show staged scenes.
